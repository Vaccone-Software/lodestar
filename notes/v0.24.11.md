Lodestar holds its tongue while you are on a call, and answers a script
when you are not.

An offer is the only thing this app says without being asked, and there is
one moment when saying anything is unforgivable: while your screen is being
shared with other people. Chips became draggable and clickable last
release, which made them more visible than they have ever been, and made
this worth doing properly.

The calendar was already half of the answer and could not be the whole of
it. It knows about the meeting you accepted, exactly, with a start and an
end, and knows nothing at all about the call that started thirty seconds
ago in a huddle. So the microphone answers the other half: being on a call
means something has the input device open, and that is a question macOS
will answer without permission and without naming who asked. Lodestar never
opens the device and never listens to anything. It asks whether the light
is on.

Being wrong in one direction costs a suggestion arriving late, and the
coach speaks twice a week and is in no hurry. Being wrong in the other
direction puts a chip on a screen a room full of people is looking at. The
whole design follows from that asymmetry: when in doubt, hold.

What holds is what Lodestar starts. The coach waits, and a chip a clicked
link would have left waits, and both are taken down if they were already
standing when the call began. What you asked for still happens: a link you
clicked still opens and still lands where the rules say. And the meeting
chip is exempt, being the one surface that is about the meeting.

The microphone holds the floor for a long time and not forever. An open
device has no end the way a meeting does — it ends whenever the app that
opened it decides to, and some never do — and the failure that would
produce is the quiet kind, where offers do not break but simply never come
again. After four hours the microphone stops counting on its own, and a
call that genuinely runs longer has a calendar entry that keeps holding
the floor.

**And the verbs are scriptable.** `lodestar go g` summons the chain.
`lodestar web example.com --profile brave:Xonar` opens a destination.
`lodestar layout undo`, `lodestar breath gb`, and `lodestar state`, which
answers with the whole world as JSON — including, for the first time, the
**parked** half of it, the windows being held as a corner sliver that no
surface has ever listed.

These are not a second implementation of anything. Each one ends in the
same call a keypress makes, so a scripted summon and a typed summon are
the same event in the same log. They are recorded as coming from somewhere
other than your hands, which is what keeps the graph's own numbers honest
about which addresses you actually use.

It reaches the running instance through a socket in your own directory,
readable by nobody else, because a verb needs the window model and the
accessibility grant that only the running instance has. Worth being plain
about what that is: any process running as you can drive it, and can
therefore arrange windows using Lodestar's grant rather than its own. The
bound is deliberate. It can go where you can go and arrange what you can
arrange, and it can do nothing Lodestar itself cannot do. Nothing behind
that door reads a file, runs a command, or types into an app.
