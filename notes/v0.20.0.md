The tutorial no longer tells you about Lodestar. It watches you use it.

The old walkthrough was a full screen deck: eight cards, some forty facts,
every key explained before any key was pressed. It owned the keyboard while
it was up — carefully, behind a dead-man's guard, because a tutorial that
eats keys once locked a machine — and it still taught very little, because
reading about an instrument is not holding one. It never said which
physical key is lode. It never said what Lodestar is for.

The walk replaces it. A small card sits in the corner of the screen and
asks for one real thing at a time: hold the lode key until the map appears
— the card names the key and lights it on a drawing of the bottom row.
Summon an app through the launcher. Press one of your own letters. Put
hint letters on a window and put them away again. Open Ask, open the
clipboard history, open the cheat sheet once so it becomes a place you
have been. Each card advances the moment the engine sees the gesture
actually happen. There is no next button; the doing is the advancing.

The card is never key. It takes no keystroke, holds no focus, and cannot
interrupt: type, switch apps, walk away mid-step, and it waits. The whole
class of tutorial-ate-my-keyboard bugs is gone by construction, and the
keyboard-interceptor machinery that used to manage it carefully is gone
with it. Skipping is per step — lode ⌫, the coach's own "not this one" —
and the walk is never dismissed, only finished or left for later: an
unfinished walk resumes where it stopped, on the next boot or from How
Lodestar Works in the menu bar. While the walk is up the coach stays
silent, because the two share the lode-lode grammar and an assent must
only ever mean one thing.

A new machine meets the door first: one small centered card, workable by
mouse, asking for the one permission. While you are inside System Settings
it parks at the edge of the screen and stays readable — the deck used to
hide itself for the grant, leaving you alone in the pane with the
instructions gone. And the door tells the truth the deck did not: a second
permission exists, Screen Recording, for selecting text you can see, and
Lodestar asks for it the first time you use that feature, never at the
door.

If your graph holds fewer than four apps, the walk drafts letters from
what is running and offers them — two quick taps of lode takes them,
written through the same path ⌘K uses, so the file and the walk are the
same source of truth. A built graph belongs to somebody who already knows
how to add letters, and the offer stays home.

Everyone sees the walk once, including everyone who sat through the old
deck. What the deck taught was not this.

Scroll moved: lode ` opens it now — the corner key the idle left hand
finds without looking while the right thumb holds lode. The comma is
freed, and it does nothing yet on purpose: one day it should mean
settings, the way ⌘, does everywhere else on the Mac, and no habit should
have to be unlearned on the way there. A double-tap bound to scroll
follows the move on its own; scrolling itself is unchanged.
