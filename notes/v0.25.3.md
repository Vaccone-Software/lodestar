Nothing you can see changed. What changed is how sure we are of it.

The coach's chip shares one pane of glass with the chain guide and every
flash, and the question of who has that pane at any moment turned out to
be the most dangerous question in the app: the one real bug the coach has
shipped was a chip erased by the next chain three hundred milliseconds
after it appeared, and billed as read ten seconds later. The decision had
been made in the one part of the app no test could reach — the wiring
between the engine, the panel, and the coach — where a list of "effects
that take the glass" was missing the single effect that draws nothing.

That wiring is now one named call, and there is a harness on it. The real
engine, the real panel, and the real coach are stood up the way the app
stands them up, on a world that records what it was asked and never moves
a window, with every wait running on a clock the test owns. Keystrokes go
in as synthesized events through the tap's own callback, so a scripted
`lode s` walks exactly the path a hardware one does, verdict included — a
chip is standing, a chain completes, and the test asks two things: what
the glass shows, and what the coach believes it shows. Eighteen scenarios
hold those two accounts together: the chain, the miss, the peek, the
launcher, a bare flash, window work that must leave the chip alone, the
read at ten seconds and the expiry at sixty, both gestures and both of
their refusals, and the order of the voices that share the lode-lode
grammar. Reopening the old hole fails the first of them on the spot.

Two small consequences ride along. Every delay a surface takes — the
peek's half second, the settle, the minute a chip stands, a flash's fade —
now goes through one door, which is what let the harness own time; and the
coach's ledger stamps its events from that same door rather than from the
wall, so what it records and what it decides can no longer drift apart by
a clock.
