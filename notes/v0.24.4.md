The coach stops charging for chips you never saw.

A chip and a chain guide share one panel, and the sharing was never the
problem: the guide is the state your hand is waiting on, the chip is a
suggestion that can wait, and the chip should be the one to yield. What
was missing was the record of the yielding. Every completed chain quietly
orders that panel off the screen, and because that effect draws nothing
it had never been counted among the ones that take the glass — so the
chip vanished while the coach went on believing it stood, and ten seconds
later spent one of its three showings on it. Against real navigation the
arithmetic is not close: fewer than half the chips the coach has ever
counted were still on screen to be counted.

Three things change. Taking the panel down is a takeover like any other
now, so a chip erased by your next chain costs nothing and comes back
later instead of being quietly billed. An offer is spent only if the chip
is still the thing on the glass — which is what the code has always said
about itself and had never once checked. And a boundary no longer paints
on the instant: it waits for the hand to come to rest, because the moment
after a navigation is the moment you are most likely to navigate again,
and a chip drawn into that is a chip drawn to be erased.

Counts already in your ledger are left alone; nothing retroactively
decides what you did or did not see. If the coach has been quiet, it may
have been talking to an empty screen — from here it waits for a real one,
speaks a little less often, and is there when it does.
