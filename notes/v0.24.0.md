Hints are reborn on the pixel sensor, and the two lenses become one
machine with two doors. `lode ;` and `lode /` now enter the same mode —
same capture, same recognizer, same stitching, same grammar — and the
key you entered through declares what a capital does: at the select
door a pick anchors a span, at the hints door a pick clicks. Lowercase
always aims, a capital always fires, in every application alike.

The grammar changed, and your hands should hear it plainly: **a label
now fires with shift held** — `⇧letter` clicks — because bare
lowercase was given to aiming, which is the whole prize. Type a few
characters of anything you can read, matches light up and wear chips,
and the capital clicks the word you picked. Right-click moved too:
**`⌃⇧letter`**, the system's own modifier for a secondary click, and
on a two-letter chip the last key decides the button. This is what
makes clicking work where the old hints went blind — a terminal, an
Electron app, a dense page — because clickability is no longer asked
of the application's accessibility tree. You read it, you typed it,
you clicked it.

The tree still serves where it is honest. Before you type, every
pressable the tree can name wears a corner-pinned chip immediately, so
a dialog's three buttons cost one capital and no typing, and icon-only
controls stay reachable. Wherever a pick lands inside a tree-known
element, the click is the app's own press rather than a synthetic one;
text inputs focus, putting your typing where you were headed. Sticky
`lode ⇧;` still chains: every fire re-reads the window after a beat.

The old tree-only hints controller and its overlay are gone — what
survived is exactly what the door borrows, the harvest and the press —
and the mode finally counts itself: every session records how it was
aimed, how it fired, and how long the entry chips were read before the
first key, which is the number that will decide whether they stay.

Six hundred ninety tests stand under all of it.
