The clipboard arrives.

⇧⌘V opens a strip at the corner of the screen: what you copied most
recently runs along the bottom under the home row, and five pinned
slots climb the left under the number row. Press a letter and it
pastes. The right angle is the point — a pin and the thing you just
copied sit at the same corner, so there is one place to look instead
of two ends of a wide bar.

⇧ and a label paste the clip exactly as it was copied, with whatever
formatting the source app offered. ⌘ and a label open that card's
actions: pin it, delete it, save an image to Downloads, or never save
from that app again. `/` searches, and the matches stay under the same
letters. Escape leaves, and so does a click, and so does ⌘ with
anything that isn't a card — reaching for ⌘S means you have moved on,
and a strip sitting invisibly over someone else's shortcut is the one
state it should never be in. That keystroke is swallowed rather than
forwarded, because a repeated ⌘W costs a keypress and a forwarded one
costs a window.

Pins are slots, not a list. Unpinning leaves a hole rather than
renumbering the survivors, so slot 2 means the same thing next week as
it does today. That is the whole reason to have them: a paste you can
make without looking.

What it will not remember matters as much as what it will. A password
manager marks its clip concealed precisely so tools like this look
away, and Lodestar refuses those before reading a byte of them — not
merely before writing. You can exclude an app entirely, or any clip
containing a phrase you name. Nothing is recorded from an app you have
excluded, and turning the clipboard off in the config stops the
recording rather than just taking ⇧⌘V away. The history lives in
`~/.local/share/lodestar`, readable only by you and kept out of Time
Machine, because `~/.config` is what people commit to dotfiles
repositories and a clipboard history has no business in a git remote.
`lodestar clipboard clear` erases it, running instance included.

Images paste into terminals now. A pty carries text and cannot carry a
picture, so ⌘V into a terminal was never going to land one however
rich the pasteboard was. But a path is text: an image bound for a
terminal is written out as a PNG and its path is what ⌘V delivers, and
the tool on the far side reads the picture back off it. Graphical apps
never see this and still get the image itself.

The surfaces that draw a key and a name are one family again. The
clipboard's actions, the chain guide, the searcher's rows, the ⌘K
graph card and the cheat sheet had drifted into three chip shapes,
three keycap sizes and three icon sizes; they now read from one set.
Menus lead with an icon and a name and keep the key at the trailing
edge, the way a menu is read, while the cheat sheet still leads with
the key, because it is the one surface you scan by key. Destructive
actions are red and sit below a rule in every menu that has one. Click
hints are a point larger.

Underneath, copying got considerably cheaper. A large screenshot is no
longer decoded to ask how wide it is, nor decoded and redrawn to make
its thumbnail, nor written to disk on the main thread while you wait —
the size comes out of the file header, the thumbnail is downsampled
straight from the source, and the bytes are filed on a background
queue while the strip draws immediately.
