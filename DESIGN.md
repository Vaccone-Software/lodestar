# Lodestar: Design Specification

> A keyboard driven macOS window navigation and layout system: one instrument replacing a window manager and a launcher.
> Named **Lodestar**: the fixed star you steer by. Every window you care about becomes one.

## Core philosophy

Destination over process. Every action names where you want to be and takes you there in one stateless gesture. No sequential traversal, no cycling, no ambient modes you can be stranded in. The goal is not "fast navigation," it is navigation that compiles into motor memory and stops being navigation. Every act is a stable address you summon.

Test for any feature: does it become a fixed gesture the hand owns, or does it force a fresh decision every time? If the latter, it does not belong.

## The addressing axis (the spine)

Every primitive addresses one of two things, and this distinction is forced by the nature of each:

- **Apps** are stable, launchable, singular. A key can always resolve to an app, and can always relaunch it. Addressed by: **launcher** and **graph**.
- **Specific windows** are instance-bound. The whole point is to pin _this_ Ghostty window out of ten. A window is not durable, cannot be freely recreated, and its identity can churn across close/reopen. Addressed by: **breaths**.

Consequence: app-scoped primitives (launcher, graph) are the safe bedrock. Window-scoped primitives (breaths) carry the real technical risk and depend on window-identity stability. Build the bedrock first.

## The four primitives

### 1. Launcher (the entry point)

A simple fuzzy-match app launcher. This is how an unaddressed app first enters the system; the other primitives are what you graduate things into from here. It also subsumes any "menu of all apps," so no such menu is needed.

- Plain `enter`: focus-or-launch the app, maximized.
- `shift + enter`: open the app beside the current layout.

(v2 refinement, deferred: launcher may surface individual windows, not just apps: show the app when there is one instance, show windows when there are several. Decide this by using it, not by reasoning now.)

### 2. Graph (permanent apps)

`lode` + a chain of letters resolves deterministically to an app or a sub-target of an app.

- Single app: `lode S` -> Slack.
- Subdivided app: `lode B P` / `lode B G` / `lode B X` -> Brave profiles (personal / Google / work).

Focus-or-launch: if it exists, focus it; if not, create it. Always maximized. The extra letters compile into muscle memory and stay deterministic. Cycle detection on the graph. App-scoped.

### 3. Marks (retired in 0.9.14)

Marks were letter addresses for **specific windows** — the temporary tail of
things not worth promoting to the graph. Shipped on `` ` ``, removed once the
design that followed had absorbed the need: the graph addresses apps (including
browser profiles) and nests arbitrarily deep, so rare destinations live one
letter further in rather than in a second namespace; `lode ⇥` reaches the other
windows of an app; and breaths address saved arrangements. What marks alone
could do — pin one live window out of ten — never justified a second register
to remember or the premium key it held.

The lesson kept: a mark bound a **live window ID**, so it died whenever its
window closed. Any future address for "this specific thing" must survive the
thing being gone, the way a breath relaunches what it needs.

### 4. Breaths (saved layouts)

A breath is a **snapshot** of a specific-window layout. It is not a mode. You compose a layout live (with shift, below), then save it, and can return to it later.

- `lode B` + a letter chain: save / return to a breath (addressed like the graph, e.g. `lode B G B`).
- `lode B B`: update the latest breath to the current layout. (Reserves the `B B` path from being an address.)
- A snapshot does not auto-update. To edit: add a window with shift, or remove one by closing it, then `lode B B` to commit. To revert, re-summon the breath.
- Persistence: session-first is guaranteed; cross-restart is best-effort (relaunch the parent apps, reposition as well as macOS allows). Breaths pin specific windows, so restore is inherently best-effort.

Breaths pin specific windows, so they carry the window-identity risk in full.

## Ask (added after the four)

`lode ⏎` opens a second bar with its own grammar, on the axis the four primitives do not address: a destination **inside** an app. The four are about which window you are looking at; Ask is about which browser profile a URL lands in, which is the same question one level down.

It stays a separate bar rather than rows in the launcher, and that separation is the design. The launcher answers "which app," and mixing web destinations into it would put two kinds of answer in one list, forcing a read-and-reject on every query. Two bars, two grammars, no ambiguity — the cost is one more gesture to learn, paid once.

Three kinds of row, and the third is why it works: **links** (a name you typed for a site), **domains** (anything that looks like a destination), **searches** (everything else). Every row wears the profile it will open in. Profiles resolve by precedence — a link's pin, then a `web.routes` pattern, then `web.fallback`, then the browser you were in last — and every surface that shows a profile also shows _which_ of those decided it, because an inferred answer can change tomorrow and a chosen one cannot.

Routes are the piece that earns its keep quietly: a pattern matched against whatever you typed, host or query alike, so `x.com` always opening in Personal is one line of config rather than a habit.

They also reach **links clicked in other apps**, because that is the half of the problem that needs them: when you type into the bar you already had the chance to choose, and when you click a link you had none. Lodestar can stand as the http handler and apply the same table. That lives in the main binary rather than a helper app — a second download is a worse product when the rules are already configured here — and the design that makes it safe is inversion: **pass-through is the behavior, a matched rule is the exception**. The common path is the empty one, so a link cannot be lost by code that does nothing, and nothing changes for a user who never writes a rule.

Three constraints hold that path, each load-bearing. It touches config and LaunchServices only, never the window model, because a blocking AX call would let one wedged app delay somebody's link by seconds. It never places the window: a link someone sent you is not a request to rearrange your layout. And it never asks LaunchServices to open a URL without naming the target app, which is what stops the dispatch handing the link straight back to us forever.

Because the app updates itself daily, the update watchdog gained a functional gate: while Lodestar holds the browser role, a successor must prove its router answers, not merely that it booted. A window manager that cannot place a window is annoying and obvious; a build that cannot route a link fails silently, in other people's apps, on a machine where every link now depends on it.

Test it passes: the gesture is fixed (`lode ⏎`, type, `↵`), and the profile decision is removed from the moment of use rather than added to it.

**The diagnostics log carries none of it.** Not a URL, not a host, not a typed query — the log is paste-able (`lodestar diagnose` tails it), and a list of everywhere you went is not diagnostics. Where something opened is what a bug report needs. The observation layer is the one place a destination's _host_ is kept — local, bounded, and only because a host that always lands in the same profile is one route recommendation away from being config (see "What the app learns" below); the path and query stay nobody's. Config edits log the _name_ you chose, never the destination.

## The clipboard (added after the four)

`⇧⌘V` puts history on screen: recents along the bottom labelled by the home row, pins climbing the left edge numbered 1–5, the column drawn through the highest slot in use and one free slot after it. Both meet at one corner, so the two things you are most likely to want — a pin, or what you just copied — are one hot region rather than two ends of a wide strip.

The strip is **never key**: it reads its keys from the event tap, so the window you were typing in keeps focus and its insertion point, and the paste is a plain `⌘V` into an app that never lost the cursor. History lives in `~/.local/share`, not `~/.config`, because the config is what people commit to a dotfiles repo and clipboard history is the last thing that belongs there. `clipboard.exclude` / `exclude-apps` are how something never gets recorded in the first place.

A clip is **whatever was copied**, not the first piece of it. The pasteboard carries items — three files selected in Finder are three of them — so a history that kept only the first would hand back a third of a copy while looking like it worked. And while the search band has the letters, `⌥` is what addresses a card: the chips stay on the cards and wear the modifier that makes them true, because a label you can see and cannot press is worse than no label.

**A card is found by what it shows and where it came from.** An image says what it shows: the pixel sensor reads it once, off the main thread, a beat after the card exists, and the text under the size line is what the search reads — a screenshot of an error is found by the error. A browser copy carries the page it was made on, and the card wears its host beside the app's icon, searchable as a second address, because "the one from GitHub" is how a hand remembers a clip that never says so. And a restore is not a copy: a clipboard manager putting the previous contents back after a paste of its own marks them, the card is already in the history, and promoting it would reorder the list for something the hand never copied — so it stays where it was. The strip records itself the way every surface does: how the card was addressed, what the search cost, how it ended, never the clip; and pasting from it is a channel in the overhead ratio, against two keystrokes.

**Every input pastes.** The launcher, the commands bar and Ask are real text fields in key panels, and AppKit routes ⌘V through the main menu's key equivalents — which an accessory app does not have until it makes one. It has one now, unseen, carrying only the editing chords. The strip's band, select and the click door read their keys off the tap, so ⌘V there is the pasteboard's text folded to one line and fed whole: into the band as query, into select as the search — an identifier pasted lands on a settled world exactly as one typed would.

**A card opens in the draft.** `E` in a card's actions puts its whole text in the draft, standing above the strip with the card lit beneath it and the pin column stepped aside, silent, the cursor at the top — a card is read from the beginning, and a quarter of text cards run past the five lines a card draws, one in ten past the preview cap entirely. It is the draft with no microphone: opening one flips a Bluetooth headset to its telephone profile and pauses music, and a stray `lode .` over something being read must never do that, so the door has none and `lode .` inside it says so. The panel grows to the display's visible height and widens only when the longest line asks, so prose stays at reading width and code gets its columns. Two ways back, both to the strip, and neither touches the pasteboard: `⏎` saves changed text to the card in place — same position, same pin slot, same age — and `esc` keeps changed text as a new card with the original left alone, because an edit lost to a reflexive escape is the worse failure; unchanged text writes nothing either way. A lode verb, a ⌘ chord the draft does not take, a click elsewhere, or the strip's own toggle ends the door the way escape does and takes the strip with it, which is the strip's own bargain. The strip stays the only paste surface: read a card large, then paste it, is `⇧⌘V` `⌘A` `E` `⏎` `A`. The door is three keys from the strip and stays there until the record says it is common; a modifier on the address now would be guessing.

Two smaller things ride with it. A card that holds more than it shows says so beside its age — lines for prose and code, characters for the one long line a URL is — so the hand knows a card is worth opening before it opens it; a card that shows all of itself stays bare, because a signal on every card is furniture. And the search is measured at every exit: how many cards answered the last query, how many recents were on show, how many exist, and how deep a card pasted from the search sat, never the query. The record so far showed search typed twice and abandoned both times, and nothing in it said whether search fails to find or fails to show; this is what will.

## Select (added after the four)

`lode /` addresses the one thing on screen that never needed an assigned address: text is made of the characters the keyboard already produces, so its address is itself. Type a few characters of what you can see; matches highlight and wear capital chips; a capital anchors the start, a second search and capital anchor the end, and the span between them — in document order, whichever way it was placed, so no concept of direction exists — becomes the selection.

The split that makes it clockless: lowercase and symbols always extend the search, capitals always pick a chip. Labels are letters only, so `⇧4` is `$` and can never be a pick; matching is case-insensitive, so a real capital is never needed. This composes the two instruments the product already owns — incremental search and prefix-free hint chips — and inverts the arithmetic that kills pure hints on prose: a screenful of words outnumbers any label alphabet, but two typed characters collapse it to a handful.

The mode always ends the same way — **the span highlighted, the next verb the user's** — because a gesture that did different things on different text would put a read-the-situation step inside a fixed gesture, which is the one thing the design forbids. The first cut auto-copied read-only spans with a flash — one keystroke saved, at the price of the mode becoming the arbiter of intent; real use rejected it within a day.

That ending is reachable one pick early. The start anchor lands on the whole word rather than the characters that named it, so it is already a selection someone can see, and `⌘C` there takes it and ends the mode exactly as a finished span would: copied, still highlighted, the next verb yours. A second anchor is what a _span_ needs, not what a _word_ needs — and most of what a hand reaches for is one word. The mode still never decides that for anyone: the user asks with ⌘C, which is the auto-copy lesson kept rather than relearned.

**Uniqueness commits.** When the typed search narrows to exactly one match on screen, the pick lands without the capital: a keystroke whose only legal meaning is confirmation carries no information, and the grammar stops charging for it. This is not the auto-copy mistake returning — that cut arbitrated _intent_, and uniqueness arbitrates nothing: the pick was fully determined, and the next verb stays the user's. The hand is usually mid-word when it fires — it planned a word, not a chip — so the word's unclaimed tail is absorbed as confirmation: letters that continue the picked word extend nothing, and the first letter that could not be the word's next one starts the far end's search, whole. The same rule closes the span, and the shell goes on absorbing the end word's tail while the highlight stands, because those letters would otherwise type into the app beneath — or replace the native selection they had just made. Two doors refuse it on principle: the click door, where a pick is a click and an action must never fire itself on uniqueness; and the replay of keys buffered while the sensor was still reading, because a match unique in a partial world is not unique on the screen. `select.commit-on-unique` pins the capitals back.

One character never commits. A single-character search matches standalone tokens only, and app chrome is full of icons the recognizer reads as lone letters — an F beside Report, an O beside Autofill — so its uniqueness belongs to the screen's stray glyphs, not to the hand. Type _Software_ and the S was unique on the page, so the anchor landed on an icon; then _of_, a substring of the word, was unique too, and the span ran from the icon to the word the hand was still typing. Measured by typing every visible word on a live page through the grammar (`probe selectsense`), one word in five anchored wrong or ran away, every one of them on a single character, and a two-character floor took both to zero; it is the floor, in both stages. Uniqueness is also asked only of a settled world: the fast recognition pass is a sketch, a few lines short and rough around rare glyphs, so it shows chips and commits nothing, and the accurate pass, landing under a search already typed, commits the anchor its next keystroke would have — a hand that finished typing before the sensor finished reading is not charged a capital for it. The accessibility tree keeps its own uniqueness only where it is the only sensor. And a capital arriving while the end word's tail is still pending is the pick the hand had planned before uniqueness made it, swallowed once, because passed through it replaced the selection it had just made.

**The sensor is the screen itself.** The first architecture harvested accessibility trees, and real use taught its lesson in one evening: trees build lazily, un-build after idle, answer geometry per app per element per mood, and draw element boundaries a span cannot cross. Pixels have none of those properties — the Vision framework reads a capture of the focused window and places any character range to the pixel, identically in a terminal, a browser, and a chat history. Consistency is the whole point of the mode, so pixels are primary; the Screen Recording permission is requested lazily on first use, and the accessibility harvest remains the permission-free fallback.

Accessibility's demotion is to the two jobs it is actually good at. **Grounding**: recognition of clean screen text fails only by single glyphs (`1`↔`l` — reproduced in our own tests), harmless in prose and catastrophic in an identifier, so a pixel-sensed copy is repaired against the accessibility-read truth when the two align. **Committing**: a sensed span that lives verbatim in the focused editable element becomes a true `AXSelectedTextRange` — the app's own highlight, every editor verb. Everything else ends as the held highlight, ⌘C serving the grounded text.

Fragmented text is stitched before matching (`SelectRuns`) — recognized lines merge exactly as accessibility leaves did: same line joins with the gap the layout drew, lines of one column join with newlines, and a span across fragments highlights as one honest rectangle per piece. A line belongs to the frontmost window under it and a run never straddles two — the reference window beside the one being read is its own text, whatever column the two share. System overlays at screen-saver level and above own nothing: they are invisible by design, and a notch companion's window was found claiming fourteen lines of the page beneath it.

Stitching stays conservative on purpose, because an over-merged run promises spans across unrelated columns. Conservative is not blind, though, and for a while it was: the merge only asked whether a line continued the line just before it, and reading order interleaves columns line by line, so on any screen with a sidebar the line before was the other column's, the column test failed every time, and both columns shattered into single-line runs (85 recognized lines became 58 runs on the first page measured, 44 of them a single line — most of that was this). Now every run stays open for the whole pass and each line joins the nearest run it continues: a card is one run, a column is one run, and a feed beside a sidebar that stitched to 92 runs stitches to 43. The rules did not loosen; they are simply asked of every run that could answer.

Still, the span, not the run, is what had to cross: it takes the tail of the run it starts in, everything between in reading order, and the head of the run it ends in — what a drag down the page would have taken. Confining the far end to the anchor's own run was the alternative, and it put an invisible wall at the end of the line the eye happened to start on; the wall was the bug users hit, on GitHub, in a diff, in any chat log. And _between_ means between on the page, not merely between in the list: the two ends define the column the span runs down, and a run beside that column — the sidebar's next section, the neighboring card — is not between them, whatever its place in reading order. Ends in different columns cover both, so crossing is still allowed; it has to be asked for at both ends. Every piece is lit, and the copy is exactly the lit pieces joined by newlines — the highlight is the whole promise, so it may never be a summary of one. No app can be asked to select across its own element boundaries, so a span that crosses always ends as the held highlight, never a native one.

**What leaves the clipboard is read twice.** Pixels aim; they must not be the last word on a copy, because recognition wobbles on the confusable glyphs — `1` `l` `I`, `0` `O`, `rn` `m` — and a silent `l` for `1` in an identifier is the worst kind of wrong. So a held span's copy is made deliberate. The pixels are served at once, so ⌘V is never early. Then the app is asked to select the same span by a synthetic drag, a press inside the first glyph and a release inside the last, and to copy it; when its text agrees with the pixels up to the confusables, the app's copy is the truth and the mode ends natively, the app's own highlight the ending. The drag never lands on a control — both ends are checked by role first, because a press on a button is a press — and a drag that grabbed a neighboring span disagrees and is overruled. The pointer is walked to the first glyph before it presses, and walked home afterward rather than warped, because some apps take a press without a position: Ghostty's is wherever the last move or drag left it, so a bare press selected from the pointer's old resting place, a warp told it nothing, and the same span had to be selected twice, the first time to put the pointer in place. Every press Lodestar makes with the pointer walks first — the click door's fallback click and its right-click ride the same scripts (`SyntheticPointer`), so the rule is decided once, where a test can read it. And the span is read a second time from the frozen frame, cropped and upscaled with the recognizer's alternatives consulted and numeric tokens repaired by shape, which serves when the app could not. The first architecture's reasons for not dragging still hold for the interface: the drag is a truth source, not the gesture.

Test it passes: the gesture is fixed (`lode /`, type what you see, a capital — then `⌘C` for that word, or a second type-and-capital for the span), and the decision the mouse used to make with a drag is made by reading, which the eyes were already doing.

## The draft (added after the four)

`lode .` opens the draft listening; `lode ⇧.` opens it editing — the same insert-mode surface, one with the mic writing into an empty buffer, one silent with the field's text pulled in. A third door opens from the clipboard strip, `E` on a card, silent with the card's whole text; it ends in the card rather than in an app, and is described with the strip. It is text in flight toward an app: not a file, not a buffer you return to, a thing you speak or type into that lands where your cursor already was. The name is a placeholder that has earned nothing yet.

**It is the fifth bar, and the first you stay in.** The launcher, Ask, commands and the strip are passed through in a second; the draft is open for a sentence or a paragraph. What keeps that from being the ambient mode the design forbids is that the panel is the mode — there is no state without glass on screen — and it has exactly two exits. `⏎` pastes. `esc` in insert mode goes to normal mode; `esc` in normal mode closes. Nothing persists after either except a clip.

**Never key.** The draft reads its keys off the event tap, the clipboard strip's architecture, so the app underneath keeps its cursor and its selection the whole time. `⏎` is a plain ⌘V into that cursor. Nothing is focused, nothing is refocused, and the app's own enter is never pressed: the draft pastes, sending is the next verb and it is yours.

**One cursor, two inputs.** Typed characters land at the cursor; the recognizer's settled words land there too, joined by a spacing rule so a typed identifier and a spoken sentence read as prose. The recognizer's volatile words draw dimmed after the cursor as a ghost — and the moment the hand starts typing, the ghost becomes text on the spot, reserved where it stands, with the typed characters landing after it. The final that arrives later revises the reserved words in place, so dictate, type, dictate lands in the order it happened. (A first draft had typing land before the ghost, which put every interjected word ahead of the sentence it interrupted; and while reserved words await their final, cumulative volatiles are held back rather than drawn twice.) Backspace takes the grain of the last input: a character after typing, a word after speech, because a transcript's unit of error is the word and a keyboard's is the character. The ghost is never touched; it settles on its own.

**Posture is set, never toggled.** The mic writes only in insert mode. `lode .` means the mic writes; `lode ⇧.` means it does not; pressed inside the bar they mean exactly the same, whatever was true a moment before, and neither changes the editor's mode. A toggle would have to be read before it could be pressed, which is the fresh decision the design forbids; a setter is idempotent. The vim `i` and `esc` move between modes without touching the mic, so entering normal mode silences it and `i` brings it back.

**The destination is live.** `⏎` lands in whatever is frontmost at that moment, and the register line above the text says where that is as it changes, with the app's icon. So the draft can be opened anywhere, spoken into, carried to Slack with ⌘⇥ or `lode S`, and landed. Lode gestures pass through the bar to the engine untouched; ⌘ chords pass through to the system, except the few that edit text (⌘⌫, ⌘←, ⌘→, ⌘V, ⌘C, and in normal mode ⌘Z, ⌘⇧Z, ⌘A as the editor's own verbs), which would otherwise land in the app underneath and edit the wrong thing. Another bar or mode opened over the draft — the launcher, hints, the strip — borrows the keys while it stands and gives them back when it closes; the draft waits underneath, because two exits means two, and pressing `lode ;` to click into the field you mean to paste into is exactly the case. A graph chain released with the draft open ends, and its letters are text. Pinning the destination at open was considered and rejected: it has a nice trick in it (glance at another app, knowing the text still goes home) and a system built on it is inconsistent every time the trick is not wanted.

**What is under the cursor comes along.** A selection in the field is pulled in through either door and replaced at `⏎`, but only if the destination is still the origin field; anywhere else it is a plain paste, and nowhere it is a copy. The edit door pulls the whole field when there is no selection, where accessibility hands it over and it is under a size cap, and replaces it by selecting all first. With no focused field at all, `⏎` leaves the text on the pasteboard and says so.

**Both endings keep the text.** Every exit puts the text on the pasteboard, so it is a clip in the strip and `⇧⌘V` recovers it; the clipboard's excluded-apps rule applies to the destination, which is the frontmost app, so a draft into an excluded app leaves no trace. The draft always opens empty (or with the pulled text, or with the card's): a bar that opened differently depending on history would be a read every time. The panel grows upward with the text to the display's visible height and scrolls past it, one rule for every door.

**Vocabulary is repair, not hope.** `draft.words` holds the names speech gets wrong. They are offered to the recognizer as context, and the draft repairs settled tokens itself: a token within a short edit distance of a word, first letter fixed, length within one, becomes the word, case and all. Short words get case repair only, because at four or five letters a one-edit neighbor is usually a different real word. The probe found Apple's `SpeechTranscriber` ignores contextual strings and `DictationTranscriber` honors them but hears worse; the repair pass is what makes the better model usable with a vocabulary.

**The input is named, and it is chosen.** The register line says "opening the microphone" from the door until the recognizer reports listening — on a Bluetooth headset that is one to three seconds of profile flip, and words spoken into it are gone — then names the device the recognizer is actually reading and draws its level, because a silent microphone is indistinguishable from a broken feature until something says which one it is: the first evening's "the mic does not work" was a closed lid and a system default input with no signal. The name is a menu — "System (Cypress)" says what the system default currently is, and any input can be chosen from it — and the microphone glyph beside the meter is a button that turns the mic off and on. These are the panel's only two mouse targets, and they are the two things a hand at the keyboard cannot decide from the keys: which device, and whether to be heard right now. Both wear macOS's own recording orange. `draft.input` is the config line the menu writes (also in Settings, under Interaction); empty follows the system default. An audio engine is bound to the device it was created for — changing the device under a kept engine leaves its formats stale and delivers nothing — so a different device gets a new engine and the same device keeps the warm one.

**Music steps aside.** Opening the microphone on a Bluetooth headset drops the whole device to its hands-free profile — mono, telephone-band, a separately remembered volume — because the classic radio cannot carry music out and voice in at once. No app can prevent it and every app that opens the mic causes it, so while the draft listens there is nothing worth hearing: whatever is playing is paused, and resumed once the output is a music profile again (the nominal rate rising back past the telephone band; three seconds as the backstop). The players are asked directly by Apple Events — Music, Spotify — because the audio system cannot answer the question: a first design inferred "playing" from CoreAudio's process roll call and pressed the Now Playing ⏯, and measurement killed it twice on this machine — a paused player keeps its silent output stream for fifteen seconds, and a keyboard-click app holds one forever, so the roll call reads silence as music and a blind toggle starts music into a silent dictation. Asking is exact both ways: pause is pause, play is play, only what said "playing" is owed a resume, a hand that pressed play first settles the debt, and a browser tab that cannot be asked is left alone. Derived, never configured — it acts only when the input about to be read and the current output are the same Bluetooth radio (UID roots match; the default-following engine's private aggregate wrapper is seen through to the default it wraps), which is why there is no setting: the condition is the knob, and a pinned non-Bluetooth input never opens the gate.

**On-device, and the record keeps counts.** Recognition runs on the Mac (`SpeechAnalyzer`, macOS 26), the microphone grant is asked at the first `lode .` and never before, the model loads at boot only once the grant exists, and no audio is kept. The observation record holds, per draft: the door, how it ended, the destination app, seconds open, characters typed, words settled, backspaces, mode switches, and the waits to the first word and the first key. Never the text.

**One grammar, no picker.** The editor is vim, and there is no setting that makes it helix or anything else. A grammar dropdown re-spells every key, so every surface that speaks a key — the footer, the walk, the sheet, the tests — would have to speak them all, forever; and helix is not a keymap but a different machine (selection-first), so honest support is a second editor, not a table. The population that is not vim-shaped is served by the floor instead: both doors open in insert mode, which speaks the one dialect macOS teaches everyone (arrows, ⌥ and ⌘ moves, ⌃a ⌃e ⌃k ⌃w ⌃u), and vim exists only past an esc a person need never press. The editor stays a pluggable pure machine behind a small effect seam, so if the observation record ever shows real hands asking for another grammar, one can be built then — the option is kept by architecture, not by a knob.

**Normal mode is vim.** `Vim` (LodestarCore) is a pure state machine over the buffer: motions, operators, counts, text objects, find, replace, join, case, undo, redo, dot repeat, and the visual modes, tested without an app. Stock vim first; this user's own extensions come after, additively, on stock's weak spots. Two deliberate deviations, decided before the first line: `⏎` commits in every mode (`⇧⏎` is the newline; a NeoVim hand already uses enter for jumps), and only `y` and `p` touch the system clipboard, so `dd` then `p` pastes what was yanked and the strip holds yanks and drafts and nothing else. A third came from use: `j` and `k` walk the lines the eye sees — the panel's wrapped lines, asked of the layout that draws them — because draft prose makes a logical line an accident of width; an operator's `j` stays logical, so `dj` keeps meaning what vim's `dj` means. The text objects carry two imports from mini.ai, the user's own editor: `q` is any quote and `b` is any bracket — covering pair first, innermost, then the nearest ahead, an empty interior last — with `b` deliberately taken from vim's parentheses, because one key that finds the pair you mean beats three that ask you to name it. Surround speaks the same dialect — mini.surround's, not vim-surround's: `sa` wraps a motion, object, or the visual selection, `sd` unwraps, `sr` swaps delimiters, with `q` and `b` as finders and an opening character padding with spaces; vim's `s` was only `cl`, and the trade was made in the user's own editor first. Find motions are document-wide without wrapping: `f`, `F`, `t`, and `T` cross line breaks, and the moment one is pending the first instance of each reachable character lights in the macOS accent color — the letters themselves recolored and weighted, never a wash behind them, because vibrancy composites a background wash on this glass into nothing. The lights go out the moment the hand acts; after the jump, the cursor is the answer. The draft is set in the system's mono face: a block cursor in a proportional face is a new width on every character, wrapped columns lie under `j` and `k`, and a lit letter's weight would reflow the line — mono makes all three constant, because its advances survive the weight by design. `esc` clears a pending command before it means anything else, leaves visual mode before that, and only then closes.

Test it passes: the gesture is fixed (`lode .`, speak, `⏎`), and the decision the hand used to make — which app, which field, whether to fix the third word or re-say the sentence — is either removed (the cursor was already there) or made with the keys it already owns.

## Promotion: ⌘K on the thing in front of you

The bars share one editing gesture. `⌘K` acts on the selected row and offers only what that row can become: an app joins the graph, a domain becomes a named link or a route, a link can be routed or removed, a route can be removed from any row it sent somewhere. Every card shows what `↵` would commit before it commits it, and refuses with the reason rather than silently.

This is how things graduate. The launcher is where an unaddressed app first appears; Ask is where an unnamed destination first appears; `⌘K` is the one move that turns either into an address, and it writes straight into the config so the file and the UI are the same source of truth. Editing by hand and editing by card produce identical bytes.

The decisions live in `WebMenu` (LodestarCore), tested without an app; the panels only draw and translate keystrokes. Any future card belongs there too — a state machine wearing an AppKit coat is still a state machine.

## The chord: letters under one hold compose

Lode is the one key this product holds; every letter is a tap. So the phrase is the hold: while lode stays down, the first letter takes the stage as any summon does, and every letter after it joins beside — `lode G B` is Ghostty beside Brave, `lode G B S` three columns, and lifting lode ends the phrase. It is the beside verb spoken without the shift, and it spends addresses the hand already owns, so there is nothing new to learn; the state it reads is the thumb, the way shift is, so nothing about it depends on what is on screen. Digits and breaths keep their meaning under a hold, since `lode 1 2` reordering a layout would be a different verb, and a chain still chains: `lode G E O` is Ghostty beside Outlook. The whole phrase is one undo step. What it deliberately does not change is the summon: a plain summon of a member already standing in a split still replaces, so moving inside a split is the digits' job and the next letter summon of anything collapses the split back to the resting posture. The record counts each composition, how many summons the split survives, and a chord undone within two seconds — the correction made under one hold that argues against the chord, if it turns out common. A simultaneity chord was rejected first: a 40 ms window sits inside a typist's 150 ms variance, and a hand attending to timing is the cost the chorded-keyboard literature measures. Test it passes: `lode G B` is one motor program, and the decision it removes — which breath letter, whether to reach for shift — is exactly the kind that kept the beside verb at 0.4% of placements.

## Shift: the universal "beside me" modifier

One rule, applied everywhere. Plain invocation of any app-opener (launcher, graph) means "take me there, maximized." The same invocation with `shift` means "bring it beside my current window." The new window opens to the right or below the active one depending on orientation.

This is the only mechanism that composes multiplicity, and it does so in the moment, with the intent declared in the gesture and gone immediately after. There is no compose mode to forget you are in. Shift-open repeatedly to build three or more windows.

## Layout and index navigation

- **Resting posture**: one app, maximized. Multiplicity is always something you explicitly invoked (shift, or returning to a breath). You can never be confused about what you are looking at.
- **The sweep**: a plain summon makes the resting posture true of the screen, not just the layout — every window _this session has placed_ that is still standing on the active display parks with it, silently, in the summon's own undo step. Only session-claimed windows, ever: a window Lodestar did not summon stays exactly where its app put it (so a dialog can never be slivered — nothing summons a dialog), and the strays behind a transparent terminal are wallpaper's problem, not Slack's. The stragglers macOS placed are one `lode 0` from joining the system. Any outside road to a parked window — cmd-tab, a Dock click — unparks it on the spot, so nothing Lodestar hides can ever be lost to a road Lodestar does not own. An earlier sweep (a `lode 0` verb that parked every non-member) was retired for having to guess what was safe to move; this one moves only what it was once asked to move, which is why it needs no gesture, no setting, and no guess.
- **Equal sizing**: windows in a multi-window layout are equal-sized.
- **A window that cannot fill its slice is centered in it.** System Settings has a fixed width, a palette its own size; asked for the whole display they take what they can, and the slice's origin pinned them to its top-left corner. Now a member that came back smaller than its slice on an axis is centered on that axis — read a beat after the moves, when the frames say what each window actually took, and again from the corrective pass, which is what catches an app that answered late either way — so a summon that cannot fill the display lands in the middle of it, and a beside-summon in the middle of its column. Only smaller: a window larger than its slice is left where it is, because centering it would push part of it off screen, and the corrective pass already absorbs it along the layout's axis. The digits read a window's center along that axis rather than its left edge, since a narrow window centered in the top of a stack has an edge to the right of the wide one under it, which would have put the top window second.
- **Index navigation**: `lode` + a number jumps to a window by index, left-to-right then top-to-bottom.
  - `lode 1` = leftmost / topmost. `lode 9` = rightmost / bottommost (Chrome-style: always the last, even if fewer than nine).
  - Cap at nine windows, because the digits are the addresses: a tenth made `lode 9` ambiguous — the "last" it promised was a window no digit could name. Beyond nine is not usefully navigable; the tenth beside-summon is refused with the reason, the world untouched. Prevent the instance rather than support it.
  - `0` is likely redundant (1 already covers first). Leave unassigned for now.
- **Close behavior**: closing a window in a multi-window layout retiles the survivors to equal-sized. This preserves index order, which protects the muscle memory built on it.
- **Orientation**: commands to flip a layout between all-horizontal and all-vertical.

## Readability

Every word Lodestar draws is set on one scale, and the scale has a floor. Nine sizes chosen one at a time, from 10 to 23, had drifted in over a year, and the smallest sat below what a hand that lives at a screen should be asked to read: a card's caption at 10 point in tertiary grey measured 2.2 to 1 against its glass, less than half the 4.5 that reading needs. Now there are three sizes and one for the launcher's field — meta at 13 for captions, keys and footers; body at 16 for what a row, a card, or the draft says, which is the size a terminal is set to; title at 18 for a bar's name — and the same three on the settings window and the walk, since an eye is one eye. Tertiary grey is kept for what is decoration or absence (an empty pin slot, a separator, a placeholder) and retired for anything read; secondary measures 6 to 1 on the panels' charcoal, the label colour 13. The block cursor is a solid plate with the glyph cut out of it, the inversion every terminal draws, and the insert bar is the system's own insertion colour at three points, because the cursor is the thing the eye hunts most. A card says "3m ago" rather than "3m", one word that makes the letter mean a time to someone who has never seen the card, and names beside its icon the page it was copied on or the app that made it. The ratios are read from screenshots over the harness's fixed ground, not from the eye. And there is one glass: every panel, card, and chip is the launcher's backdrop — regular liquid glass with the equalizer scrim inside it — differing only in the veil's weight where state asks (a lit card, an empty slot), never in material. The clipboard's cards and the hint chips once wore a second recipe, clear glass under a heavier scrim, and clear glass has no frost, so a card's opacity was entirely the scrim's; the scrim read the material's stamped tone once, on a cold panel before the material had sensed its backdrop, and the clipboard opened see-through the first time and right every time after. The scrim now re-reads whenever the stamp changes, and frost carries the opacity anyway, so a wrong first guess is a shade rather than a hole. The one rule deliberately not adopted is opacity by dwell time — a surface the eye rests on could stand on a heavier scrim than one it glances at, and in practice that is a glitch, and inconsistency is worse than the measure.

## Technical approach

- **Language**: Swift. Binding directly against the C Accessibility APIs, no bridge tax.
- **Move / resize / focus**: public Accessibility API (`AXUIElement`).
- **Window identity**: one private, read-only call, `_AXUIElementGetWindow`, to bridge an AX element to a stable `CGWindowID`. This ID is the durable handle breaths pin to. Mature tools in this space rely on exactly this one private call and nothing else.
- **Hide**: park the window off-screen, beyond the union of all display bounds (must account for multi-monitor so parked windows do not peek onto a second display). A parked window stays live and its `CGWindowID` is unchanged; toggling back is one position change (milliseconds).
  - `cmd H` app-hide is a clean nice-to-have for the app-scoped case (removes an app's windows entirely), but it is per-application, so useless for hiding one window among several.
  - Minimize-to-Dock is a rare fallback only: it animates, is slower, and restores less reliably.
- **SIP stays ON.** The single private call does not require disabling it. Native Spaces manipulation does, so do not touch Spaces at all.
- **Tone**: every surface follows the system appearance — one dark look, one light look, chosen by the user's own setting and never by the pixels behind a panel. Liquid Glass wants to adapt its tone per panel to the backdrop; that is how two panels a foot apart once resolved to opposite tones, and how text and ground once landed on the same color. We keep the frost and take away the material's vote: an equalizer scrim senses the material's choice and drags it to the system's tone (`Glass.swift`). A surface summoned for a second must cost zero reorientation, which means it must look the same every time — the gesture test, applied to the eye.

**It comes back.** A navigator that dies takes the keyboard's roads with it, so above every feature stands one promise: a lodestar that crashes is running again within seconds, without a hand touching anything. The login agent carries `KeepAlive` on unsuccessful exits — and the subtle failure was never the plist, it was provenance: an instance started by the updater, Finder, or a rollback runs as an unsupervised application job, so every self-update quietly traded the promise away (found when a crash on `j` left the machine ownerless). Now any boot that finds the agent idle hands the session back to launchd and bows out through the pid-file takeover, so the resident process is always the supervised one. A run that dies uncleanly is named in the next boot's log, an in-flight draft is stashed half a second behind the hand and comes back on the pasteboard, and the editor itself is storm-tested — every key, operator, target, and text object, plus seeded storms through the real panel — because the draft is the surface the hands trust with anything, and trust is exactly what a crash on a motion spends.

## Dependencies

Do not depend on existing window management libraries; this project _is_ that layer, and carrying foreign abstractions would fight the design. Instead:

- Learn the hard-won lesson of prior resolvers up front: raw AX calls are synchronous and block on the target app's event loop, so one hung app can freeze the whole switcher for seconds. A cached model is the answer. Learn this trap early rather than rediscovering it painfully.
- One cross display truth: to move a window across displays, set size, then position, then size again, because macOS clamps sizes to the current display.

Own everything else.

## Build order

**Slice 0, the probe (throwaway):** Before any UX, validate window-identity viability. Enumerate windows, get their `CGWindowID`s via the private call, park one off-screen and pull it back, and confirm the ID survives a close-and-reopen for the actual target apps (Brave, Ghostty, ProtonMail). Green light = IDs stable and reposition works, so window-scoped addressing is viable. Red = it demotes to session-only. This one result could reshape everything above it, so it is the first code written.

**Slice 1:** Launcher + focus-or-launch, maximized. This alone is a usable app switcher, and it is the resolver every other primitive sits on.

**Slice 2:** Graph, on the same resolver.

**Slice 3:** Marks (since retired — see above). First real window-identity risk, isolated on top of a foundation already trusted.

**Slice 4:** Breaths. Specific windows plus layout.

**Later:** Layout undo/redo (a nicety with no downside). Counts and dot-repeat on close/resize verbs.

## What the app learns (the observation layer)

Lodestar watches how you reach things — locally, deletably, off by one config line — so that one day it can coach: not dashboards, one earned sentence at a time. The architecture is decided by one lesson: **aggregate at read time, never at write time**. v1 stored a median per chain and the median turned out to confound chain length with fluency; the mistake was frozen into the data. So the source of truth is now an append-only event log (`events.jsonl`, a rolling year, sharded by month so closed months are never re-parsed and retention retires whole files), and everything else — the summary file, every fitted model — is a view over it, rebuildable when the models improve. What is kept: pauses tagged by position and whether the map was up, abandons with their hover, the wrong key actually pressed, each road's measured price in seconds, app→app transitions, hosts and the profile they landed in. What is never kept: window titles, URL paths and queries, clipboards, launcher queries beyond two characters.

Two additions ride the same rails, each with its own discipline. The **health pulse** (`observations.health`, its own switch because it watches more than Lodestar's gestures) folds all input into one event per quarter hour: keystroke and click counts, scroll _bursts_, active minutes, inter-key rhythm moments, and the backspace rate — the correction key being the one classic early strain signal. The hard line is structural and stated where the code can read it: **never key identities on general typing** — per-key or per-digraph timing on arbitrary text statistically reconstructs content, so the fingers' metrics are global moments and one anonymous flag, forever. The **click pulse** rides beside it at one grain finer: clicks per quarter hour per app, with the clicked element's accessibility role class and whether the hand had just left the keys — an app name and a role, never a label, a title, a coordinate, or a URL, so "button in Brave" is kept and "the Submit button on checkout" is structurally unreachable. It exists because the pulse is app-blind by design and the coach cannot price a pool it cannot name. None of it is judged anywhere: the pulse feeds a mirror (the printout now, the retrospective later), and a mirror describes. And the **latency events** are the instrument observing itself — pane discovery, retile batches, any surface's warmup — so a regression in the instrument surfaces exactly the way a regression in the hand does. The monthly rollup archives all of it as sufficient statistics, plus weekly health sub-rollups, because a monthly histogram cannot show the week everything changed and weeks are the unit the curriculum already thinks in.

The analysis side holds itself to three disciplines. A pause is judged against what that chain's _shape_ costs this user's own hands (position and digraph regressed out), never against a constant. A recommendation must clear a decision-theoretic gate — probably worth more time than it costs, relearning priced from the user's own curves — and survive false-discovery control across everything tested at once; thin data self-suppresses through wide uncertainty rather than through magic thresholds. And every config edit is treated as a natural experiment (epoch-stamped, curves restarted), because a single-user, no-A/B design gets its causality nowhere else. Models run in shadow, scored on one-step-ahead prediction, and earn influence only by out-predicting the naive baseline — complexity lives here so the surfaced product can stay one sentence.

The printout carries the product's own score: the **overhead ratio**, actual input cost against a keystroke-level floor per channel — navigation and select timed by the instrument, typing and pointing priced from counts at the hand's own measured constants, and every line saying which it is. A channel at 1.0 has nothing left to give; everything above it is backlog. Beside it stands the **pointing pool**, the clicks ranked by app and role class and priced the same way — the standing answer to what the keyboard should learn next, computed rather than audited.

Two more columns the layer could not see until it looked. **Roads**: every focus change is charged to the hand's most recent act — a Lodestar route, the system's ⌘⇥, a click, or nothing the tap could name — so the transition matrix can finally say how a switch happened and not only that it did (`FocusRoads`; the road tags the focus event, never the app that came before it). **Backspace runs**: the one named key, now counted in bursts — single, two to four, five and more — because a single is a typo and a run is a sentence being retaken, and only the second is anyone's business to shrink. Still no key identities; a run is a length, not a letter.

**Predicted warming.** Chromium and Electron tear their accessibility trees down after idle minutes, so the first hints or select after a switch used to pay for a tree that could have been standing. The warmer already fires on focus; now it also fires _before_ the switch, for the apps most likely to be next (`NextApp`, `Prewarmer`). The prediction is a first-order chain blended with recency — the app just left is the single most likely next app, and the chain carries the rest — with its two numbers tuned on the user's own stream and every strategy scored in shadow, predict-then-observe, in the engine printout: on the stream this was built against, the blend reached 60 / 82 / 90% for the top one, two, and three predictions, so three warmings cover nine switches in ten. The cost is kept where it cannot be felt: one attribute set per app, throttled to one per half minute, after the hand settles, on a utility queue behind a quarter-second messaging timeout, only for apps with a live window — and a slow batch is written down as a latency event, so the instrument's own price stays visible.

**The universal redirect.** A coach accept was the only move the product could see, because only the ledger knew where an address went; a hand edit, a ⌘K retarget, or a settings change moved the same binding and left the old gesture a plain miss. The epoch diff (`GraphEpochs`) now names a move — one binding removed and its target added at exactly one new address; two of either is ambiguous, and an ambiguous signpost is worse than a miss — and the old address says where it went by the same rules an accepted supersede obeys: until the new curve bends, nine weeks at most. No road to a binding change may change a gesture cold.

Test it passes: the user never sees a model, only `lodestar observations` (the plain printout that makes the store consentable) and, occasionally, one actionable line whose expected value has already paid for the interruption.

## The coach

The coach is the observation layer's voice: one priced, evidence backed suggestion at a time, offered at a moment the user can verify the claim from their own immediate experience. It is named the way the launcher and the graph are named — a register, never a persona. No first person, no praise, no mascot: advice is legitimate coming from a coach, and every step past the label toward a character raises social expectations a tool can only disappoint.

**The chip** rides the HUD's glass, never takes key focus, and captures nothing bare: its whole grammar lives on the lode key. Tap lode twice to accept — assent addressed to the instrument, structurally unable to collide with typing. Lode ⌫ answers "not this one". Ignoring it is the third answer: decay is "later", never a decline, so the chip carries no decision pressure. A missed offer parks in the menu as an inbox of at most one. Accepting writes exactly one config line through the same path ⌘K uses, so the file and the chip are the same source of truth — and anything that cannot be one config line stays in the report.

**When it speaks:** at navigation boundaries, because a navigation is the user declaring a context switch — the one moment interruption is nearly free. Suggestions whose evidence is a felt cost prefer their cue (the bind offer lands seconds after the search it would remove), but a cue is a scheduling preference, never a permission: a suggestion with no cue, or whose cue does not come within days, goes out at any quiet boundary. Vetoes are absolute — a live camera, an open bar, a chain in flight all silence it.

**Acceptance buys airtime.** The channel's quiets — after an answer, between offers, the days a cue-having suggestion holds out for its cue — are scaled by the kind's record, and on top of that by the run of accepts: each accept in a row halves them, to an eighth after three, and one no puts the book rate back at once. Two offers in seventeen days, both taken, with ten findings standing, was a coach quiet for a record that had earned the opposite; the evidence gates are untouched, because this is pacing, never permission.

**The curriculum** is the object being optimized, not any single suggestion: one learning habit at a time, and the next releases when the current one's curve bends, not when a calendar says so. Pace is gated by demonstrated capacity — progressive overload — so the motivated compound quickly and a slow month is not nagged through. Declines are graded and none is forever: "later" retries at later moments then parks, "never" sleeps for a season, and both reopen when the evidence materially outgrows the answer, because the data kept moving. Every answer feeds the curriculum's own calibration; the coach's response to no is to get better, not louder. Calibration is now literal: each recommendation _kind_ is scored on what its accepts actually did — an accept whose curve bent is a win, a "never" a loss, an accept still learning neither — and history multiplies the ranking and, through the pacing scale, the channel's two quiet clocks, never the evidence gates. Evidence decides what may be offered; track record decides what is offered first, and how often the asking may happen: a kind whose accepts demonstrably bend curves waits out shorter quiets, down to half, and a kind that keeps losing waits longer — the coach's response to no is to get quieter, not louder. The per-suggestion retry leash keeps the book rate, because it prices the user's attention rather than the kind's credibility.

**The coach reshapes, not just says.** The evidence (Grossman et al., CHI 2007) is that closing the old path is what makes hands switch and a purely visual nudge tests below doing nothing — so the chip's job narrows to consent, and the intervention itself is structural wherever one exists. Supersede was the first: an accepted address replaces the one it beat, with a redirect that expires when the new curve bends. The **rebind** joined it by reading the hand's own errors as proposals: the wrong key names the address the hand believes in, the recovery seconds later names the destination it wanted, and when the recoveries agree the offer is to bind that destination at the address already being pressed — closing the old one the same supersede way. The **flatten** reads the failure gradient instead: bare keys misfire at essentially zero and prefixed chains measurably do not, so a chain whose starts keep failing — its own stumbles plus its completion-weighted share of the prefix's — is offered the same supersede to a free letter, every misfire's recovery in the price. Where a rebind needs the wrong keys to agree on one letter, the flatten fires on diffuse failure: no single belief is wrong, the depth itself is the tax, and the errors live in the mapping rather than the hand. And the **closed road** is Grossman's mechanism made a primitive: the nudge — an address exists and the launcher keeps winning anyway — stopped being report-only, and its accept puts a toll on the old road. The launcher, asked for that app, says which address reaches it and opens it on a second ↵; one deliberate confirm, the escape hatch priced in deliberation rather than removed. It is a ledger fact, not a config line, because a transition aid needs an expiry and config has none: the toll lifts when the address's curve bends — completions _since_ the acceptance, since the address existed before it and its lifetime says nothing about the transition — or at the same nine-week cutoff every redirect obeys. And the **breath offer** is the one accept that composes state instead of writing a line: a pair of apps that travel together arranges side by side on the spot and saves at a free letter, because past the motor floor the remaining gains are structural — eliminating a navigation beats accelerating it by any margin. It refuses rather than launches: composing must never mean opening apps behind someone. And it prices only the switches that _pulled_ a window into view — placed from parked, minimized, or hidden, or unparked for an outside road — because a pair already standing side by side is switched by focus alone, and a breath saves that nothing; the first version priced every switch and promised eighteen minutes a week for a breath that already existed.

## The guide fades

The chain guide is scaffolding, and scaffolding that never comes down is a ceiling. A map that always paints always gets read, and reading is reconstruction, not recall — retrieval is what compiles the habit. So the guide's paint waits longer as a subtree's own curves bend (up to two seconds once every address under the prefix is learned, by the same `bentCompletions` every maturity gate uses), and any stumble under the prefix — a wrong key, an abandoned chain — brings it back immediately for an hour, here and below. The weakest leaf decides, so the fade can never take the map from an address that still needs it; a fluent completion never sees the paint at all; a stall still summons help, just after recall had its chance. And disuse runs the fade backwards — the decay watch: an address the hand has not summoned in a month starts forfeiting its earned wait, a ramp rather than a cliff, and a second month of quiet erases it entirely. Retrieval decays even where the motor program survives, so the scaffold returns _before_ the stumble instead of after it. `guide.fade false` pins the old behavior, decay included.

The same law now governs every surface that answers a recallable question (`SurfaceFade`): the launcher's, the web bar's, the commands bar's, and the draft's footers of keys wait longer as the bar is used — thirty uses to the full two seconds, twice the chain's bar because a footer names several keys where a chain names one — come straight back for an hour after the bar is escaped with typing in it, and forfeit their wait with a month of disuse. Keyed by the verb the observation layer already counts, so no surface keeps a counter of its own. A hand that knows its keys never reads them; a hand that hesitates gets them after recall had its chance. Test it passes: the gesture is unchanged and nothing new must be decided in the moment — the training wheels lower themselves, and a wobble puts them back.

**Software that shrinks.** A gesture the hand has not fired in ninety days becomes a finding: the report names it with the one config line that would retire it, and the cheat sheet draws its row quiet with the same line in its label. Nothing turns off on its own, and no chip ever carries it — chips are priced in seconds and this saves none — so the offer lives where the eye already reads, and the accept is the line. The graph is never called dormant; it is the spine. Ninety days is the threshold for saying, never for doing.

**The seam has a harness.** Every decision the coach makes lives in `Coach` and is tested one at a time, and the coach's first real bug lived in none of them: it lived in the wiring between the engine, the glass, and the coach, where "who has the panel" was decided by a list one effect had fallen off. That wiring is now one call (`SurfaceWiring`), and a scenario harness (`LodestarAppTests`) runs the real engine, the real panel, and the real coach through it — synthesized keystrokes fed to the tap's own callback, every wait on a clock the test advances by hand — and asks, at each moment, what the glass shows and what the coach believes it shows. The two must agree. Pure tests keep the decisions honest; the harness keeps the seams honest, and any future surface that shares the glass earns a scenario there before it ships.

Test it passes: the gesture is fixed (tap lode twice, always assent, no offer means no-op), and each appearance is rare enough to mean something — silence remains the resting state.

## Open questions (resolve by prototype, not reasoning)

- **Window-identity stability** across close/reopen for the specific apps in use. The probe answers this.
- **Namespace reservation**: `M`, `B`, and the digits are reserved as first characters (marks, breaths, index — marks and breaths since moved off letters entirely). Apps whose natural first letter is one of these need another path in the graph. Note in particular that Brave (`lode B ...`) currently collides with the breath prefix; one of them moves.
- **Launcher windows vs apps**: whether the launcher surfaces individual windows (v2).
- **Index `0`**: what, if anything, it does.
- **Naming the register**: "link" is the plain word, deliberately not Raycast's "quicklink". Whether a distinctive noun (beacon, waypoint) earns a config migration is unresolved; the plain word ships until it does.
- **Noticing what you keep typing** — resolved in the observation layer's favor: hosts are counted, locally and bounded, and a host that always lands in one profile becomes a _route recommendation_ rather than a hint chip. The chip question (where such a nudge appears in the UI) stays open; the data question is settled.
- **The retrospective**: the coach's slow mirror — a quarterly, offered-not-shoved look back at what the hands learned and the minutes returned, Wrapped-shaped, in glass and the house voice. Waits on a quarter of ledger existing to speak from; the per-suggestion closure receipt was considered and rejected (transactional framing crowds out the quiet transformation the product is for).
